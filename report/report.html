How to read this report

Every job on After AI is scored against four questions. They are not a score out of twelve and they are not a grade; they are the four things that have actually kept work out of a machine's reach so far. The work needs a body, in a place that keeps changing; A person is legally or morally answerable for the result; It depends on a relationship with a person; The role is one job, not a pile of tasks.

Each job also carries a verdict — survives, at risk, mixed, emerging, or possible — and the verdict is part of the reading: an AI-native job scores low on protection because it never needed protecting, which is not the same as being exposed.

Nothing in this report is a forecast of our own. The numbers are transcribed from the studies named beside them, and where a study measures exposure or applicability rather than job loss, the caption says so. The full bibliography is at the back.

Survives · care · 2025–2035

Home health and personal care aides

The largest source of new jobs in the US economy — and the one AI helps least.

Well protected — Most of these hold. A machine cannot do this work on its own, so the job keeps its shape.

The four tests — how much each property protects the job: not at all · a little · yes · very much. More protection, not more importance.

  • yes A person is legally or morally answerable for the result
  • very much It depends on a relationship with a person
  • yes The role is one job, not a pile of tasks

The numbers

  • +18.1% Projected growth, 2025–35 [BLS 2026]
  • +847,300 Jobs added [BLS 2026]
  • $35,800 Median annual pay [BLS 2026]
  • 760,500 Openings a year [BLS 2026]

What the evidence says

  • Adds more jobs than any other occupation published by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics. [BLS 2026]
  • About 760,500 openings a year, most of them replacing workers who leave rather than new positions. [BLS 2026]
  • AI's documented value in care is documentation, scheduling and monitoring; experts across the care workforce reject it as a substitute for physical assistance or human relationships. [ACL 2026]

What holds

  • Hands-on physical assistance: lifting, transferring, bathing, gait belts
  • The work is a relationship, and the client can tell the difference
  • Licensing, liability and dignity keep a human accountable for the outcome
  • Demand is driven by demographics, not by software adoption

What could still go wrong

  • Median pay is $35,800 — a job-quality crisis, not an AI story
  • Most openings replace workers who leave, not new growth
  • Charting, scheduling and vitals logging will leave the human's plate

The close reading

It is the least fashionable answer and the biggest one. Home health and personal care aides add more jobs than any other occupation the US Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks — 847,300 of them to 2035, about one in every seven jobs the economy is projected to create.[1]

The reason is not that the work is unskilled. It is that the work is unbundleable. A caregiver lifts a person out of a chair, notices a change in how they are breathing, and is trusted not to steal from them. When the care workforce was asked directly where AI helps, the answer was documentation, scheduling and monitoring — and the answer to "can it replace this?" was no.[2]

Two honest caveats. Median pay is $35,800, and most of those openings are replacements for people who leave, which means the growth is partly a measure of turnover. If AI takes the charting and the scheduling, the occupation gets more physical and less paid — the wrong direction for job quality even as headcount rises.

The numbers

  • +41.0% Projected growth, 2025–35 [BLS 2026]
  • +137,800 Jobs added [BLS 2026]
  • $132,300 Median annual pay [BLS 2026]

What the evidence says

  • The fastest-growing occupation in BLS projections to 2035, at 41%, against 3.5% for all occupations. [BLS 2026]
  • Providers are relying more on these roles to deliver care under collaborative, team-based models. [BLS 2026]

What holds

  • Prescribing and diagnosing means legal accountability, which cannot be delegated to a model
  • Care teams are being restructured around these roles, not away from them
  • The clinical relationship carries the outcome; a wrong call is a harmed patient

What could still go wrong

  • Documentation and routine triage shrink the administrative half of the role
  • Training pipelines still depend on supervised hours that AI is not replacing

The close reading

The durability here is not physical, it is legal. Someone has to sign the prescription, carry the liability, and make the judgement call at 3am when the protocol does not cover the case in front of them. That is the definition of work where a human stays in the loop because removing them transfers risk nobody wants.

It helps that AI lands on the administrative half. The clinical-trial evidence on AI-assisted radiology is instructive: junior radiologists' reading time fell 18.5% and report quality rose, which shifted their work from drafting to reviewing and correcting.[1] The drafting was never the job. The accountability was.

A roof is the strongest possible counterexample to the "AI does everything" story. Nothing about installing an array is stable enough to specify in advance — the layout changes when the timber under the shingle is rotten. The work is not safe because it is manual; it is safe because it is variable and a mistake injures someone.

Firefighters

Ranked the single most AI-proof occupation in a study of O*NET physical abilities.

Well protected — Most of these hold. A machine cannot do this work on its own, so the job keeps its shape.

The four tests — how much each property protects the job: not at all · a little · yes · very much. More protection, not more importance.

The numbers

  • $59,280 Median annual pay [Resume Now 2026]
  • #1 Rank on Resume Now's list [Resume Now 2026]

What the evidence says

  • First on a ranking of occupations that rely most on six O*NET abilities: stamina, static strength, reaction time, spatial orientation, time sharing and problem sensitivity. [Resume Now 2026]
  • The distinguishing feature is that the work requires recognising problems, interpreting changing conditions, deciding under unpredictable conditions — and that a wrong call injures someone. [Resume Now 2026]

What holds

  • Stamina, static strength, reaction time, spatial orientation, danger detection
  • Someone has to be accountable when the decision kills or saves
  • The environment changes faster than any specification can describe

What could still go wrong

  • Dispatch, mapping and paperwork are already being automated around the role
  • Public-sector pay and staffing are political, not technological, risks

The close reading

The one ranking here that measures abilities rather than exposure is the most useful of the lot, because it explains the pattern instead of describing it. Firefighting scores highest on stamina, static strength, reaction time, spatial orientation, time sharing and problem sensitivity.[1]

Note what that combination implies: unpredictability plus consequence. The interviewed expert's version of the argument is blunt — when something goes wrong in a life-or-death situation, "you don't necessarily want an AI agent making that final call."[2]

That is the test to apply to any role, not just this one.

Radiologists

The profession everyone predicted would vanish first, which instead got better at its hard half.

Partly protected — Some of these hold. The job stays, but its routine half moves to AI.

  • not at all The work needs a body, in a place that keeps changing
  • very much A person is legally or morally answerable for the result
  • yes It depends on a relationship with a person
  • very much The role is one job, not a pile of tasks

The numbers

  • −18.5% Reading time cut for juniors [McKinsey 2025]

What the evidence says

  • In a clinical trial, AI-generated report drafts cut junior radiologists' reading time by 18.5% and improved report quality, shifting work from drafting to reviewing, validating and correcting. [McKinsey 2025]
  • PwC classifies radiologists as professionalised by AI: routine tasks automated away, human expertise emphasised, and professionalised roles growing twice as fast as democratised ones. [PwC 2026]

What holds

  • The job is a bundle: drafting reports was never the valuable part
  • Diagnostic accountability sits with a named clinician
  • AI output needs verification, and verification is expert work

What could still go wrong

  • The drafting rung of the training ladder is disappearing
  • Radiology took 20 years of warnings and did not shrink — that is evidence, not comfort

The close reading

This is the clearest case in the literature that exposure is not displacement. Expert opinion on both sides spent two decades predicting the end of radiology. What happened is in the trial data: less drafting, more reviewing and validating, and harder cases getting more human attention.[1]

It is also the template for the whole site. PwC's framework calls it professionalised — AI takes the routine work and leaves the expert work, so the role demands more skill and pays more, and those roles are growing twice as fast as the ones AI deskills.[2]

What the evidence says

Automating the individual activities (planning, marking, reporting) changes the job's texture without removing the seat. What survives is the part that needs a person in the room making judgement calls in real time.

Exposed · cognitive · to 2030

Data entry keyers

Fully specified input, verifiable output, no judgement — the pure case of automatable work.

Barely protected — Almost none of these hold, so nothing here keeps the work out of a machine’s reach.

The four tests — how much each property protects the job: not at all · a little · yes · very much. More protection, not more importance.

  • not at all The work needs a body, in a place that keeps changing
  • not at all A person is legally or morally answerable for the result
  • not at all It depends on a relationship with a person
  • not at all The role is one job, not a pile of tasks

What the evidence says

  • Data entry clerks appear on the World Economic Forum's list of fastest-declining job roles to 2030. [WEF 2025]
  • The ILO's highest exposure gradient is dominated by clerical roles: data entry clerks, typists, accounting and bookkeeping clerks and general office clerks. [ILO 2025]
  • In Microsoft's analysis of real AI usage, the most common and most successful AI-assisted activities are information work — creating, processing and communicating information. [Microsoft Research 2025]

What holds

  • The task is the whole job
  • Every step is already codified in a system the machine can reach
  • Nothing is lost by removing the human

The close reading

If you want one role that explains the risk side of this site, it is this one. Every property that protects the jobs above is absent here: no body in a variable world, no accountability, no relationship, no indivisible bundle. The job is the task.

Both of the independent exposure measures converge. The World Economic Forum's employers put data entry among the fastest-declining roles to 2030.[1] The ILO's task-level scoring puts clerical work in the top exposure gradient, and Microsoft's analysis of actual AI use finds information processing is what the tools are already best at.[2][3]

None of that is a forecast of a firing date. It is a description of a task profile with nothing on the defensive side of the ledger.

Exposed · cognitive · 2021–2026

Customer service representatives

The clearest case of AI eating the entry rung rather than the ladder.

Weakly protected — One of them holds, which is usually what survives of a job after its routine half is automated.

The four tests — how much each property protects the job: not at all · a little · yes · very much. More protection, not more importance.

  • not at all The work needs a body, in a place that keeps changing
  • a little A person is legally or morally answerable for the result
  • yes It depends on a relationship with a person
  • a little The role is one job, not a pile of tasks

The numbers

  • −3.7% Employment in the most AI-exposed jobs, workers aged 22–25, year on year (September 2026) [ADP/Stanford Oct 2026]
  • +0.2% Same age group, least AI-exposed jobs [ADP/Stanford Oct 2026]
  • 36 Consecutive months of year-over-year decline in that first figure [ADP/Stanford Oct 2026]
  • 10% Entry-level share of postings in the most exposed roles, 2021 to 2026 [Indeed Sep 2026]

What the evidence says

  • In the year to September 2026, employment in the most AI-exposed US occupations fell 0.5% while the least-exposed grew 1.0%. For workers aged 22–25 the exposed group fell 3.7% and the least-exposed group rose 0.2%. [ADP/Stanford Oct 2026]
  • September was the 36th consecutive month of year-over-year decline for 22–25-year-olds in highly exposed occupations, a run that began in October 2023. For workers aged 26–30 the exposed group is now 2.8% below a year earlier, against growth of 0.9% in the least-exposed group. [ADP/Stanford Oct 2026]
  • Early-career workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations sit 19% below their kept-pace path in the August 2026 revision of the Stanford paper. That is a level gap, not the 3.7% annual rate above, and the two cannot be added. [Stanford 2026]
  • In France, permanent-contract hires for 21–29-year-olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations were about 28% below their November 2022 level by March 2026, against 8% in the least-exposed fifth. Terminations fell by about 23% and 6%, so the adjustment is slower hiring and turnover rather than layoffs. [Banque de France 2026]
  • In the most exposed occupations the entry-level share of US postings fell from 29% to 10% between 2021 and 2026, while the senior share rose from 22% to 47%. [Indeed Sep 2026]
  • Customer service representatives are among the most affected occupations in Microsoft’s usage-based analysis of Copilot conversations. [GeekWire 2025]

What holds

  • Front-line resolution of routine enquiries is exactly what agents do well
  • Volume is high and the interaction is text or voice, both native to AI

The close reading

This is where the clearest empirical finding in the field lands. Stanford's payroll-data study shows the damage is concentrated on the entry rung: 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations sit 19% below where they would be had they kept pace — 15% a year earlier — while experienced workers in those same occupations show no such gap.[1]

The monthly update of the same dashboard, published 21 October 2026, turns a snapshot into a run. In the year to September, employment in the most AI-exposed occupations fell 0.5% while the least-exposed grew 1.0%. For 22–25-year-olds the exposed group fell 3.7% against a 0.2% rise in the least-exposed group. September was the 36th consecutive month of year-over-year decline for that group: the run starts in October 2023, twelve months after ChatGPT's public release. It is also spreading upward — for workers aged 26 to 30, exposed employment is 2.8% below a year earlier.[2]

Two numbers on this page are easy to confuse, and adding them together would be wrong. The 19% is a level gap: where young employment sits against a counterfactual path. The 3.7% is an annual rate of change. One is the size of the hole; the other is how fast it is still being dug.

The posting data says the same thing from the other side. In the most exposed occupations, the entry-level share of US postings fell from 29% to 10% between 2021 and 2026, while the senior share rose from 22% to 47%.[3]

Outside the US the mechanism is easier to see, because measuring hires is cheaper than measuring employment. French administrative data put permanent-contract hires for 21–29-year-olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations about 28% below their November 2022 level by March 2026, against 8% in the least-exposed fifth. Terminations fell too, by about 23% and 6%, and total employment at firms reporting intensive AI use does not differ significantly from other firms. That is a hiring slowdown and lower turnover rather than a wave of redundancies — which is exactly why counting headline employment would miss it. [4]

So the loss is not "customer service is gone". It is that the apprenticeship into customer service stopped admitting anyone: three years running, and now one age band further up.

Exposed · cognitive · 2024–2025

Interpreters and translators

Ranked the single most AI-affected occupation in Microsoft's usage data.

Weakly protected — One of them holds, which is usually what survives of a job after its routine half is automated.

The four tests — how much each property protects the job: not at all · a little · yes · very much. More protection, not more importance.

  • a little The work needs a body, in a place that keeps changing
  • a little A person is legally or morally answerable for the result
  • yes It depends on a relationship with a person
  • a little The role is one job, not a pile of tasks

What the evidence says

  • Interpreters and translators rank first on Microsoft's list of occupations most affected by generative AI, based on 200,000 real AI conversations. [GeekWire 2025]
  • The ILO finds translations among the occupations that increased in exposure as model capability widened. [ILO 2025]

What holds

  • Long-form text translation is a solved problem for current models
  • The work product is text, and text is what the machine produces

The close reading

The methodological caveat belongs here, because it is the one the researchers themselves insist on. An applicability score measures *overlap between AI capability and the activities a job contains*. It is not a headcount forecast — the Microsoft authors point out that historical automation has sometimes raised employment in affected sectors, and that bank tellers survived ATMs by changing what the job contained.[1]

Read this entry as "the text half of translation is gone", not "no one will interpret a court hearing".

In flux · platform · 2021–2025

The essays

Five short pieces on what the numbers mean together: what is at risk, what survives, what to do, what is coming, and where the opportunity sits. Each is reprinted from the site with its citations intact.

01 · What survives

Work that a machine cannot close the loop on.

Four properties keep turning up in the data. A role is durable to the extent it has at least one of them — and fragile to the extent it has none.

It needs a body in a variable world. Not "blue collar" as a slogan, but physical work in environments that change faster than a specification can describe. Robots can currently reach about 13% of US work hours against 44% for software agents, because they lack fine motor control and real-time physical judgement.[1]

Someone must be accountable. Occupations are bundles, and some bundles are indivisible: every school still needs a principal, every hospital a medical director.[2] Nobody insures a model or puts one on the stand.

The work is a relationship. New tasks added to AI-exposed roles since 2022 are 2.5× more likely to depend on empathy, presence, judgement, creativity or leadership.[3] Care workers say it in their own words: AI helps with documentation and scheduling, and cannot hand someone a glass of water.[4]

The job is a bundle, not a task list. Most of today's skills apply to work that could be automated and work that cannot.[5] Radiology got less drafting and more judgement; the job changed shape rather than disappearing.[6]

02 · What is exposed

Exposure is not displacement. It is a description of a task profile.

The most important correction in this whole literature: an AI applicability score measures overlap between what a model can do and what a job contains. It is not a headcount forecast. The Microsoft researchers who produced the most-cited version say so explicitly, and point at bank tellers, whose numbers held up after ATMs arrived.[1]

What is observable is narrower and sharper. Payroll data through June 2026 shows employment for 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations sitting 19% below where it would be had it kept pace — 15% a year earlier. In levels, the two most exposed quintiles fell about 11% since November 2022 while the three least exposed grew about 10%. Experienced workers show no comparable gap. The adjustment came through hiring rather than pay, and it concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks rather than complementing them.[2]

The exposure itself is clustered. One in four workers globally is in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure, but only 3.3% sit in the highest exposure band, and clerical work dominates it.[3] Employers' own projections put data entry, clerical and administrative roles at the fast end of decline to 2030.[4]

The clearest new evidence on how this happens is a split by seniority. In the most AI-exposed occupations, the entry-level share of US postings fell from 29% to 10% between 2021 and 2026, while the senior share rose from 22% to 47% — and the tilt was about a third as large in the least-exposed occupations.[5] The same employers are hiring, just not at the bottom of the ladder.

Two honest counterweights. First, not all of this is AI: the OECD notes that the unemployment risk facing young university graduates has been rising since well before generative AI spread, which points at more than one cause.[6] Second, the overall market is not collapsing — US postings are up 0.7% year on year as of September 2026, the first positive annual reading in almost four years.[7] What is changing is who gets hired, not how many jobs exist in total.

The lab that produces the headline number now publishes it monthly, so the figure on this page is a snapshot that will move.[8]

03 · What is coming

New work arrives as a layer on top of the old work, not as a replacement for it.

Five clusters show up consistently in the 2025–26 data, in rough order of evidence strength.

Build it. AI specialist postings grew 68.9% in 2025 against 8.6% for all jobs, with a 62% average wage premium for AI skills.[1] AI engineer has been the fastest-growing US job title for two years running.[2]

Deploy it inside someone else's building. Forward-deployed engineering postings grew from 643 to 5,330 in a year, because enterprise AI projects fail for organisational reasons rather than technical ones.[3]

Feed and teach it. Data annotation and evaluation, ranked fourth among LinkedIn's fastest-growing US roles, and the one entry point that rewards domain knowledge over coding.[4]

Govern it. Roughly seven builders are hired per governance hire across the EU postings one study examined, and independent governance consultants charge $800–$2,000 a day.[5]

Supervise it. As agents take routine work, someone reviews outputs, handles exceptions, assures quality and documents decisions. AI rarely removes work without creating oversight obligations.[6] This is where most net new employment is actually landing.

04 · What to do about it

Four tests, applied to any role you are considering — plus the three ways to be wrong.

Test 1 — Does it need a body in an unpredictable place? If conditions change daily and a mistake injures someone, it is durable for a long while yet. The strongest evidence is the ability ranking: stamina, strength, reaction time, spatial orientation and problem sensitivity in settings where conditions change.[1]

Test 2 — Is a human legally or morally on the hook? Accountability roles grow as automation grows, because someone must own the output. Regulation makes it explicit: the builder cannot be the auditor.[2]

Test 3 — Would a customer be upset to learn no human was involved? Care, teaching, therapy, negotiation. That discomfort is a moat, and the data agrees — new tasks in AI-exposed roles lean 2.5× harder on empathy, presence and judgement.[3]

Test 4 — Is the job a bundle with an indivisible core? The safest jobs are the ones the org chart requires the seat for, not just the tasks.[4]

Then act on the one finding with a clear instruction in it. AI-exposed entry-level roles that were redesigned to demand senior skills grew 35% since 2019; those that were not shrank 10%.[5] The move is not to avoid AI-exposed fields. It is to enter them at the expert end, which now means demonstrating judgement years before the career ladder would once have handed it to you.

Anti-test: if the job is "produce a first draft of a document", it is already gone — not the profession, the rung. One in seven US workers has a direct skills pathway from a shrinking occupation into a growing one.[6] The jobs exist. The bridge is the bottleneck.

None of this is a forecast of which future arrives. The World Economic Forum's own 2026 scenario work sets out four plausible 2030s depending on how fast capability advances and how ready the workforce is — from Supercharged Progress to Stalled Progress — and declines to pick one.[7] The tests above are useful in all four, which is the most any advice can honestly claim.

Possibilities · labelled as speculation · Jobs that don't exist yet

Five roles nobody is hired for today, and the sourced signals behind each one. This page is our reading of the evidence, not a forecast — the difference matters, so we keep saying it.

Every other page on this site describes work that exists: someone is paid for it today, and the numbers come from a survey or a payroll file. This page is different, and it says so up front. Each profile here is a pattern with a signal behind it — something already visible in the labour market that has no job title attached yet. If the pattern holds, a title eventually follows. If it doesn't, nothing is lost by having watched for it.

The bar for inclusion: a pattern must already be visible in deployed products or hiring data, and at least one public source must describe the pressure that would turn it into paid work. What we refuse to do is attach invented salary figures or growth percentages to jobs nobody has. Where a number would normally sit, these cards say so instead.

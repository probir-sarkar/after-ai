The strongest signal here is arithmetic. One study of AI hiring across eight EU countries counted
roughly seven people hired to build AI for every one hired to govern it <Cite ids="axipro" />. The
same study is honest about its own conflict of interest — its author sells governance hiring — so we
treat the ratio as directional, not exact. But the direction is what matters: governance demand is
growing from a very low base, attached to regulation that does not evaporate when models improve.

The job, when it arrives, is closer to financial audit than to engineering: sample an agent's
decisions, test them against the rules the operator claims to follow, and put a professional
signature on the result. That is why the profile reads 3 on accountability — the entire value of the
role is a person being answerable for a judgement about a machine.

The realistic threat is not that the work disappears but that it never becomes a separate title. It
may land as an AI specialisation inside existing audit, compliance and legal professions — which
would still be new work, just not a new word. Watch for the first audit firms selling "AI assurance"
as a named practice; several have started.

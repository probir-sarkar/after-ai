Robots are the slow half of automation. Software agents already reach an estimated 44% of US work
hours; robots reach about 13% <Cite ids="mckinsey-agents" /> — but the direction is not in doubt,
and the WEF's employer survey expects robots and autonomous systems to be the one technology driver
that displaces more work than it creates, a net −5 million by 2030 <Cite ids="wef-jobs-2025" />.

Displacement numbers hide the counterpart: a deployed fleet does not run itself. It needs people who
watch throughput, clear exceptions, perform physical maintenance and decide when a machine's
judgement should be overridden. Today that work hides inside existing titles — warehouse lead,
mechanic, site supervisor. The possibility is that fleet-scale robotics concentrates it into a
recognisable role, the same way "drone operator" condensed from hobbyists into a military and
commercial profession.

The profile scores high on body for a reason: the job is at the site, with the machines, where
conditions change. That is also its defence — this is the rare future-facing role where the four
tests point the same way as the history of every previous machine fleet.

A junior analyst's week used to start with the pull: get the numbers out of the warehouse,
clean them, rebuild the deck. In October 2026 the United States added 33,000 jobs in
professional, scientific and technical services in a single month — the sector that employs
most of these roles.<Cite ids="bls-employment-oct26" /> The work did not go away. The first
eighteen months of it did.

That is the shape of this page, and it is why the verdict is *in flux* rather than *exposed*.
Demand for the analysis is holding up better than almost anywhere else in the labour market.
What has changed is what a first job in it asks of you.

### The category is still hiring

The October establishment survey is unusually clear for a sector that is supposed to be under
threat. Nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000 in a month whose prior twelve averaged 48,000, and
professional and business services accounted for 70,000 of it against a twelve-month average of
15,000.<Cite ids="bls-employment-oct26" /> Professional, scientific and technical services — the
line that holds management consulting, accounting, research and data work — added 33,000 alone,
and the wider sector has added 269,000 jobs since its low point in October 2025. August and
September were revised up by 84,000 between them.<Cite ids="bls-employment-oct26" />

The reading of that same release by the Centre for Economic and Policy Research puts the month in
a longer frame: professional and technical services had its biggest one-month gain since October
2022, and the author's conclusion is blunt — there is still little evidence of a major AI effect
on employment.<Cite ids="cepr-oct26" />

One month is one month, and the household survey beside it is weaker. Involuntary part-time work
rose by 339,000 to 4.8 million, the broad U-6 rate went from 7.6% to 7.9%, and long-term
unemployment still accounts for 27.6% of all unemployed people.<Cite ids="bls-employment-oct26" />
A strong payroll print is a good month, not a trend; the three-month average is 137,000, which is
healthy but ordinary.

### Where the losses actually are

If AI is removing white-collar work, the payroll counts say it is doing so in narrow industries
rather than across the board. Over the year to October 2026, insurance shed 89,700 jobs, a fall of
3.0%. Publishing lost 23,500, or 2.6%. Motion pictures lost 19,600, or 5.6%.<Cite ids="cepr-oct26" />
Each of those is a place where documents or images were the product and the processing was
largely rule-bound.

Attribution is the weak joint in every one of those figures. Payroll data counts jobs, not causes,
and the analysts who compiled these numbers say as much: consolidation in publishing and the
film business's own contraction are doing part of the work that gets credited to
AI.<Cite ids="cepr-oct26" /> What the data supports is narrower and still useful — the losses
that can plausibly be laid at AI's door are concentrated in a handful of document-processing
industries, and they are not showing up as a general decline in analytical work.

### What a first year now asks for

The composition change is where the evidence is strongest. PwC's analysis of over a billion job
advertisements finds that AI-exposed entry-level roles have been rebuilt to demand skills that
used to arrive after five years: 52% of the new skills listed for the most exposed junior roles
are traditionally senior ones — leadership, strategic decision-making, stakeholder management —
against 7% for the least exposed.<Cite ids="pwc-barometer" /> Roles that absorbed more than ten of
those senior skills grew 35% between 2019 and 2025. Roles that did not fell by around
10%.<Cite ids="pwc-barometer" />

Hiring managers describe the same thing from the other side. In Northeastern University's AI &
Society Survey, 8,174 people with a say in hiring were asked about AI's effect on their decisions;
of the half who said it had one, 26% reported hiring fewer entry-level candidates and 7% reported
hiring more. A further 28% said they now expect more output or higher-level work from the
beginners they do hire, and 54% use AI to read résumés — 83% in technology.<Cite
ids="northeastern-hiring-2026" /> Those are self-reports about intentions, not counted
headcounts, and they should be read as such.

Put the two halves together and the mechanism is visible. A first-year analyst who can only
produce the artefact — the deck, the pivot table, the summary paragraph — is now competing with a
tool that produces it in a minute. A first-year who can frame the question, defend the number and
explain what would change the answer is doing something the tool cannot do, and that is precisely
the work employers say they are now asking juniors for.

### The reading

The tempting story, that AI is deleting the bottom of the analytical ladder, does not survive the
payroll data: the sector is adding jobs at four times its recent average. The opposite story, that
nothing is happening, does not survive the survey data either: a quarter of the hiring managers
who use AI say they are taking on fewer beginners, and the roles that survived are visibly
harder.

What is happening is a reclassification. The junior analyst title is being kept and its content
replaced, which is good news for anyone who can do the harder version and bad news for anyone who
was counting on two years of easy work before the real job started. The honest caveat is that both
of the strongest numbers here are forward-looking — a CEO survey about the next three years, and
hiring managers describing their own intentions — while the one hard count, the October payroll
print, covers a single month.<Cite ids="pwc-barometer,bls-employment-oct26" />

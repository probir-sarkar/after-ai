The popular story about this job and the measured story disagree. The popular story is that AI
writes the code and developers are fine, because someone still has to decide what to build.
PwC's analysis of a billion job advertisements puts software developers in the **democratised**
bucket instead: the AI takes the harder tasks and leaves work that needs less expertise, with
lower barriers to entry and weaker wage growth.<Cite ids="pwc-barometer" />

That bucket is 52% of advertised roles and growing half as fast as the professionalised 22%,
lagging them by 42% on wage growth since 2021.<Cite ids="pwc-barometer" /> So this is not a
dying profession. It is a redistributing one, and the redistribution lands on the first rung:
Stanford's payroll study puts employment for 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations
19% below where it would otherwise be, software development among them.
<Cite ids="stanford-canaries" />

### What the hiring flows show

The best new evidence on that rung arrived on 28 October 2026 from Statistics Canada, and it
counts flows rather than stock. Using Labour Force Survey data, it asks what share of people in
an occupation were hired last month, sorted by how exposed that occupation is to AI. For
15–29-year-olds in coding-intensive work — software developers and programmers, software
engineers, web developers, data scientists — the hiring rate fell from 2.5% to 1.9% between the
six months to November 2022 and the six months to August 2026, and the separation rate rose from
2.4% to 3.5%.<Cite ids="statcan-hiring-2026" />

A hiring rate is not a headcount. 1.9% means that in an average month fewer than one employee in
fifty was new. A profession can hold its total employment and still quietly stop admitting
people, which is what those two lines together describe: fewer new hires, more people leaving,
roughly the same number of desks.

### The part that argues against the tidy version

Three findings in the same paper cut the other way, and they deserve as much room as the
headline. Hiring rates fell **furthest** in occupations with little AI exposure — from 4.0% to
3.3% — further than in either exposed group (2.9% to 2.5%, and 3.2% to 3.0%). Coding hiring
peaked at a fifteen-year high of 5.8% in late 2021 and began falling early in 2022, well before
ChatGPT was released. And separation rates stayed flat across every exposure group, which is not
what a wave of AI-driven redundancies looks like.<Cite ids="statcan-hiring-2026" />

So the Canadian data cannot attribute the coding decline to AI. What it can say is that the rung
thinned earliest and most in the occupation where the models are strongest — consistent with a
technology story, consistent with a post-2021 correction in tech hiring, and not yet able to
separate the two.

### The shape of the on-ramp

US posting data agrees on the shape without settling the cause. In the first quarter of 2026,
4.5% of American software development postings were entry-level and 69.3% were senior. Across
the whole labour market that spring, 46% of postings were entry-level and about 14% were senior.
Software is not a normal market with a smaller graduate intake; it is a market where the
graduate intake has nearly gone.<Cite ids="indeed-seniority" />

That gap is the verdict. The seats are still there and the work has not vanished. What narrowed
is the apprenticeship that used to turn a graduate into someone worth hiring.

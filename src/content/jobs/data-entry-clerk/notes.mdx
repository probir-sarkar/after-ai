Read a document. Put its values into the right fields of a system. Meet a rate, pass a check.
That is the work, and it is why this page scores zero on all four tests: no body in a changing
place, nobody legally answerable for the result, no relationship to hold, and no bundle of tasks
that has to stay together.

An index published in November puts numbers on the same conclusion. The ifo Institute scored 140
German occupations by how much of their work an AI system can already do at comparable quality or
faster, then tracked employment in them by age. Data entry and text processing came
top — ahead of software development, accounting offices, database and network specialists, and
journalists.<Cite ids="reuters-ifo" /> The share of automatable tasks in the most exposed
occupations is about five times the share in the least exposed, which are kitchen work, street
maintenance, painting and building cleaning.<Cite ids="reuters-ifo" />

The interesting result is not the ranking. It is what happened to the people inside those
occupations afterwards.

### The occupation grew, the entry rung did not

Since the third quarter of 2022, total employment in Germany's most AI-exposed occupations rose
1.8%, while employment in the least exposed occupations fell 6.4%. Inside the same exposed
occupations, full-time employment of 20- to 24-year-olds fell 14% — from about 279,000 to
239,000.<Cite ids="ifo-fruehindikator" />

Both things are true at once, and the second is not a rounding error on the first. Employers kept
the work and stopped using it to train people.

A second German study, on different data and a different age band, finds the same shape at a
smaller size. IAB followed about 22.3 million full-time employees month by month from January 2021
to December 2025: full-time employment of 20- to 29-year-olds in highly exposed occupations ended
3.8% below its 2022 level, against 2.6% in less exposed ones.<Cite ids="iab-forum" /> The two
figures should not be averaged — different windows, different age bands, different cut-offs between
exposed and not — but they point the same way.

### Where the machine actually gets in

The exposure score is high here for a structural reason, and it is worth being exact about it. Data
entry is not one hard task with an easy wrapper; it is a sequence of small steps that already live
inside a system: read the document, resolve the field, type the value, validate it against a rule.
Optical character recognition took the first step years ago. Language models took the second and
third — reading an invoice, a claim form or a delivery note and mapping what it says onto the
fields a database expects is the part that document-automation products promise to do, and it is
the part they increasingly deliver.

What is left is the exception. A line that does not reconcile, a field that makes no sense, a
supplier whose invoices are always wrong in the same way. Someone has to notice that and decide
what to do about it, and that is judgement — but it is a small share of a junior keyer's day and a
larger share of an experienced one's. The roles that survive inside this occupation are the ones
holding a document queue together, and those are not the roles a first-year hire is given.

### What the numbers can and cannot carry

Neither research team claims AI caused this. ifo says its indicator describes a development whose
causes could include demographic change, longer training periods and harder onboarding under remote
work. IAB puts the current downturn's effect on young employment at more than twice the size of the
exposure effect, and reports that the AI association for 20- to 29-year-olds is not statistically
significant overall, though it is in manufacturing and in occupations that do not require a
degree.<Cite ids="iab-fruehindikator" /><Cite ids="ifo-econpol-61" /> What both datasets show is an
age gradient inside occupations that are otherwise holding up. That is what a company deferring its
first hire and keeping its experienced one looks like in the totals, whatever is doing the
deferring.

### The reading

The role has always been an entry rung rather than a destination: learn the system, learn the
documents, move up. The queue is now being automated from the bottom, and the thing that used to be
the training — reading a messy document and getting it right — is what the software now claims to
do.

The occupation itself is not disappearing. Germany's most exposed occupations added jobs over the
same period in which their under-25 employment fell by a seventh. What is going is the version of
the job that consists of a rate and a schema, and with it the only on-ramp the occupation ever
offered. Anyone who wants the durable half — exception handling, system knowledge, the judgement
about which documents lie — needs a different way in, and that is the harder problem the two
studies leave open.

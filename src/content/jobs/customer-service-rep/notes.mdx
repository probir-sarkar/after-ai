This is where the clearest empirical finding in the field lands. Stanford's payroll-data
study shows the damage is concentrated on the **entry rung**: 22–25-year-olds in the most
AI-exposed occupations sit 19% below where they would be had they kept pace — 15% a year
earlier — while experienced workers in those same occupations show no such gap.<Cite ids="stanford-canaries" />

The monthly update of the same dashboard, published 21 October 2026, turns a snapshot into a
run. In the year to September, employment in the most AI-exposed occupations fell 0.5% while
the least-exposed grew 1.0%. For 22–25-year-olds the exposed group fell 3.7% against a 0.2%
rise in the least-exposed group. September was the **36th consecutive month** of
year-over-year decline for that group: the run starts in October 2023, twelve months after
ChatGPT's public release. It is also spreading upward — for workers aged 26 to 30, exposed
employment is 2.8% below a year earlier.<Cite ids="adp-canaries-sep26" />

Two numbers on this page are easy to confuse, and adding them together would be wrong. The
19% is a level gap: where young employment sits against a counterfactual path. The 3.7% is an
annual rate of change. One is the size of the hole; the other is how fast it is still being
dug.

The posting data says the same thing from the other side. In the most exposed occupations,
the entry-level share of US postings fell from 29% to 10% between 2021 and 2026, while the
senior share rose from 22% to 47%.<Cite ids="indeed-pay" />

Outside the US the mechanism is easier to see, because measuring hires is cheaper than
measuring employment. French administrative data put permanent-contract hires for
21–29-year-olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations about 28% below their November
2022 level by March 2026, against 8% in the least-exposed fifth. Terminations fell too, by
about 23% and 6%, and total employment at firms reporting intensive AI use does not differ
significantly from other firms. That is a hiring slowdown and lower turnover rather than a
wave of redundancies — which is exactly why counting headline employment would miss it.
<Cite ids="bdf-463" />

So the loss is not "customer service is gone". It is that the apprenticeship into customer
service stopped admitting anyone: three years running, and now one age band further up.

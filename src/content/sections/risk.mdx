The most important correction in this whole literature: an AI applicability score measures
overlap between what a model can do and what a job contains. It is not a headcount
forecast. The Microsoft researchers who produced the most-cited version say so explicitly,
and point at bank tellers, whose numbers held up after ATMs arrived.<Cite ids="geekwire-ms" />

What *is* observable is narrower and sharper. Payroll data through June 2026 shows
employment for **22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations sitting 19% below**
where it would be had it kept pace — 15% a year earlier. In levels, the two most exposed
quintiles fell about 11% since November 2022 while the three least exposed grew about
10%. Experienced workers show no comparable gap. The adjustment came through hiring
rather than pay, and it concentrated where AI substitutes for tasks rather than
complementing them.<Cite ids="stanford-canaries" />

The exposure itself is clustered. One in four workers globally is in an occupation with
some generative-AI exposure, but only 3.3% sit in the highest exposure band, and clerical
work dominates it.<Cite ids="ilo-index" /> Employers' own projections put data entry,
clerical and administrative roles at the fast end of decline to 2030.<Cite ids="wef-fastest" />

The clearest new evidence on *how* this happens is a split by seniority. In the most
AI-exposed occupations, the entry-level share of US postings fell from 29% to 10% between
2021 and 2026, while the senior share rose from 22% to 47% — and the tilt was about a
third as large in the least-exposed occupations.<Cite ids="indeed-pay" /> The same
employers are hiring, just not at the bottom of the ladder.

Two honest counterweights. First, not all of this is AI: the OECD notes that the
unemployment risk facing young university graduates has been rising since well before
generative AI spread, which points at more than one cause.<Cite ids="oecd-2026" /> Second,
the overall market is not collapsing — US postings are up 0.7% year on year as of
September 2026, the first positive annual reading in almost four years.<Cite ids="indeed-snapshot" />
What is changing is *who* gets hired, not how many jobs exist in total.

The lab that produces the headline number now publishes it monthly, so the figure on this
page is a snapshot that will move.<Cite ids="stanford-dashboard" />
